# LINX — Art Direction

## What the references actually do

The reference account isn't a "style." It's a **method**. Nine principles, extracted:

**1. Total genre commitment.**
Each piece doesn't reference a genre — it *becomes* one. A pharmaceutical ad is a
pharmaceutical ad, down to the "side effects may include" small print. A film poster
has a real billing block. A comic panel has real halftone. Half-commitment reads as
pastiche; full commitment reads as an artifact.

**2. The subject is never the product.**
Bones. A watermelon. A sock on a clothesline. A pill. A boxing glove. The product is
never depicted. The metaphor carries 100% of the load, and the reveal happens in the
caption or the fine print — never in the image.

**3. One hero. One idea. One word.**
CHAMP. THRILLER. QUEST. DEADHEAD. The title does the work. If a piece needs two
ideas, it's two pieces.

**4. Typography is cast, not chosen.**
Gold foil serif for THRILLER. Condensed grotesk for ADS. Blackletter script for "It
was all a dream." The typeface belongs to the genre being borrowed, not to a brand
system. **This is why the set must not look like a set.**

**5. The credit block is the signature move.**
Tiny 9pt caps at the bottom — "STARRING… DIRECTED BY… PRODUCED BY…" — is what
converts a graphic into an artifact. It implies a world. Every piece gets a version
of it: a billing block, a spec panel, a dosage table, an accession number, a colophon.

**6. Brand mark is a whisper.**
Small, cornered, unexplained. Confidence is the mark being tiny. The logo is a
signature on a painting, not a label on a can.

**7. Closed two- or three-colour palettes, held absolutely.**
That orange. That red. That cream. No gradients, no drop shadows, no "brand blue."
Each artifact commits to its own palette and never borrows from a house style.

**8. Deadpan copy. Never sales copy.**
"Cleans even the worst content mess." "Side effects may include." The wit is dry and
the reader is trusted. Nothing is explained. Nothing is exclaimed.

**9. It must survive the screenshot.**
No CTA. No arrow. No URL burned into the middle. The image works stripped of context,
because that's how it will actually travel.

## The translation to Linx

The client is not selling dispatching. The transformation is: **the work that keeps a
truck owner from driving profitably disappears.** Every concept must resolve to that
and nothing else.

### The rule that governs all 30

> The image earns attention. The fine print earns understanding.
> The caption earns the click. In that order, never reversed.

### Forbidden, absolutely

Blue trucks. Headsets. Call centres. Maps with pins. Handshakes. Route lines across
America. Chrome grilles at golden hour. Anything a stock library would return for
"logistics." Sales language of any kind on the image itself.

### The mark

`LINX` set small in a mono face, 11–13px, one corner. Never locked up with a tagline.
Never centred over the artwork. On roughly a third of the pieces it should appear only
in the credit block, and nowhere else.

### The set must not look like a set

This is the hardest and most important constraint, and it is the opposite of a normal
brand system. Twelve executions should look like twelve different studios made them.
The through-line is **intelligence and restraint**, not colour or typeface. A feed of
these should read as a curated gallery, not a campaign.

## Distribution

| Band | Count | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Pain** | 10 | Name the thing they haven't said out loud |
| **Aspiration** | 10 | Show the life on the other side of it |
| **Pattern interrupt** | 10 | Stop the scroll before meaning arrives |

Pain converts fastest, aspiration builds the brand, pattern interrupt buys attention
cheaply. Run roughly 40 / 30 / 30 by spend, and let pattern interrupt do the
prospecting.
